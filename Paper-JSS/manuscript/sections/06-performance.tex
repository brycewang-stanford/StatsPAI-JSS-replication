\section{Computational performance}\label{sec:performance}

This section reports the measured Track~C benchmark for the four
estimators marked C in Table~\ref{tab:decathlon}: high-dimensional
fixed effects (\code{sp.fast.feols}), Callaway--Sant'Anna staggered
DiD (\code{sp.callaway\_santanna}), classical synthetic control
(\code{sp.synth(method="classic")}), and double machine learning
(\code{sp.dml(model="plr")}). The goal is not to declare universal
speed superiority over the reference implementations. The study measures,
on identical inputs and tasks, whether the unified \proglang{Python}
stack imposes a cost on routine work. It also identifies where the
reference remains faster, and it makes the comparison reproducible from
the source tree.

\subsection{Benchmark protocol}

A speed ratio compares two implementations only if both perform the same
task on the same input, so the protocol fixes both before anything is
timed. Every input is drawn once, in \proglang{Python}
(\code{tests/perf/\_data.py}), written as CSV at round-trip precision, and
read by \emph{both} sides. Each side records the SHA-256 of the file it
read. The comparator refuses a row whose two sides read different bytes
or come from different runs. (Seeding \pkg{NumPy} and \proglang{R}
with the same integer does not give the same data. A benchmark that
relies on it compares different samples, and ours once did.) Outside
the timed call each side records its target quantity, so the table can
show that the two solved the same problem. Both sides run on one thread
(\pkg{fixest}, \pkg{data.table}, BLAS, and \pkg{Numba} pinned), under one
run identifier, on one machine (Apple Silicon arm64, 8 physical cores,
24~GB, macOS~26), with \statspai{} \StatsPAIVersion{}. Each result file
records the package versions, the source commit, and the 1-minute load
average at the start. Each timed step starts only after five idle
minutes. The load is sampled every 15 seconds, and a step is discarded
and rerun if the load rises above 2.5 while it runs. Times are medians over repetitions after a warmup,
reported with their interquartile range.

% Keep the four task descriptions on one page.
\Needspace*{16\baselineskip}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.25em,itemsep=0.2em]
\item[HDFE.] OLS of \(y\) on two regressors with firm and year effects
absorbed and iid standard errors, \(N \in \{10^4,10^5,10^6\}\):
\code{sp.fast.feols} against \pkg{fixest::feols}~\citep{berge2018efficient}.
\item[CS-DiD.] A balanced staggered panel of five periods with cohorts
first treated in periods 2, 3, and 4 (the same calendar on both sides),
\(N_{\mathrm{units}} \in \{1{,}000,5{,}000,25{,}000\}\): group-time ATTs
by outcome regression against the never-treated, analytic standard
errors (no multiplier bootstrap or uniform band on either side), the
joint pre-test, and the simple and dynamic aggregations.
\code{sp.callaway\_santanna} returns all four in one call; the reference
runs \pkg{did::att\_gt} and two \pkg{did::aggte} calls.
\item[SCM.] A two-factor, 30-period panel with
\(n_{\mathrm{donors}} \in \{20,50,100\}\) and the specification of
\citet{abadie2010synthetic}: one outcome special predictor per
pre-treatment year and a nested search over \(V\), weights and gap only,
no placebos, for \code{sp.synth(method="classic")} and
\pkg{Synth::synth}. The two solvers differ by design (below), and each
runs at its defaults for this specification. The package's \emph{default}
call is a different task and is reported separately.
\item[DML.] Partially linear DML with linear nuisance learners and five
folds, public-workflow construction included, \(N \in
\{1{,}000,5{,}000,10{,}000\}\): \code{sp.dml(model="plr")} against
\pkg{DoubleML} Python in the same process, so that language-runtime
overhead does not dominate a comparison of milliseconds.
\end{description}

\subsection{Measured performance results}

Table~\ref{tab:track-c-perf} reports the largest size of each benchmark
and Figure~\ref{fig:track-c-loglog} all measured sizes. In every row the
two sides agree on the target quantity. They agree to \(2\times10^{-14}\)
for the HDFE coefficient and to \(4\times10^{-13}\) for the simple ATT.
For the SCM gap they agree to \(5\times10^{-5}\), with two non-convex
solvers landing on the same solution. For DML, whose folds are drawn
independently on each side, they agree within a hundredth of a standard
error.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.25em,itemsep=0.1em]
\item \textbf{HDFE.} \statspai{} is faster at \(N=10^4\) (0.002 against
0.004 seconds), \pkg{fixest} is slightly faster at \(10^5\) (0.011
against 0.013), and \pkg{fixest} is \(1.6\times\) faster at \(10^6\)
(0.074 against 0.120 seconds), with both on one thread.
\item \textbf{Callaway--Sant'Anna.} \statspai{} is faster at every size,
by \(6.1\times\) at 5{,}000 observations and \(15.8\times\) at
125{,}000 (0.028 against 0.443 seconds). The gap widens with size, and
we have not profiled the reference to say why. The output check once caught
an error in this benchmark itself. Its \proglang{R} leg
stored the cohort as an integer column, into which \pkg{did} cannot
write the \code{Inf} it uses for never-treated units. \pkg{did}
therefore silently dropped those units and used the last cohort as the
comparison group. That leg timed a smaller, different problem.
\item \textbf{Classical SCM.} On the common ADH task \pkg{Synth} is much
faster: \(4.2\times\) at 20 donors and \(60.7\times\) at 100 (4.4 against
264 seconds). The cost is in the nested \(V\) search as \statspai{}
runs it. The outer Nelder--Mead search uses six starts (equal,
regression-based, and four random). Because donors outnumber the 15
pre-treatment predictors, each outer evaluation solves the inner weight
problem by SLSQP. Profiling at 50 donors attributes 99\% of the time to
about 5{,}000 inner solves of 19 milliseconds each. \pkg{Synth} runs one
BFGS search from its regression-based start. The six starts are there
to detect non-unique solutions (Section~\ref{sec:scm-identification}),
so a single-start comparison would time a different procedure. A faster
exact inner solver for the rank-deficient case is the main engineering
target this study exposes. The package \emph{default}
\code{sp.synth(method="classic")} uses the outcome path with \(V=I\) and
runs in-space placebos over every donor. It takes 7.5 seconds at 100
donors, of which 0.06 seconds is the fit and the rest the 100 placebo
refits. It has no counterpart in the reference call and is not in the
table.
\item \textbf{DML PLR.} With linear learners \statspai{} is
slightly faster at \(N=10{,}000\) (0.0077 against 0.0085 seconds,
\(1.10\times\)), and the interquartile ranges over 15 repetitions do not
overlap. The table calls any ratio below \(1.5\times\) a tie. The point is
only that \statspai{}'s wrapper around the same computation adds no
cost. Nothing is claimed about flexible learners, whose fitting
time dominates both.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Two of four comparisons favour the reference at the largest size. One
of them, classical SCM under the ADH specification, does so by a wide
margin. The study supports a narrow claim. On the shared tasks measured
here, the unified interface adds no material overhead to HDFE,
Callaway--Sant'Anna, or DML, and the nested-\(V\) synthetic-control
solver is the clear performance gap. The study does not support a general
``\proglang{Python} is faster'' claim. The SCM row stays in the table
because it is negative evidence the package's users need.
