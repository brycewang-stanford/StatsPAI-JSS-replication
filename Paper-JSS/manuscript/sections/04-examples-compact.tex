\section{Worked examples}\label{sec:examples}

The paper uses seven worked examples as an end-to-end smoke test of the
validated core. They are not meant as seven miniature empirical papers.
Each has an executable script under \code{Paper-JSS/replication/} that
writes the JSON, table fragments, and figures the build reads. Together
they cover selection on observables, IV, staggered DiD, RD, synthetic
control, machine-learning effect estimation, matching, and causal
time-series counterfactuals. The last example runs panel counterfactual
and interaction-effect estimators on the same bytes in all three
ecosystems, \proglang{Python}, \proglang{R}, and \proglang{Stata}. The
staggered-DiD example is the main case study. It carries one fitted
object from estimation through aggregation, sensitivity, and export. The
examples also enforce the paper's central boundary: original-data rows are claimed
only where the public extract can be redistributed or regenerated from a
reference package. Calibrated replicas are described only as structural
guards on sign, scale, and workflow shape.
Table~\ref{tab:worked-example-map} maps those examples to their scripts
and headline validation facts.

\subsection{Card (1995): returns to schooling}\label{sec:ex-card}

The Card workflow \citep{card1995using} loads the NLSYM extract, fits
OLS, 2SLS, DML, and a causal forest through the same
\code{(y, X, data)} contract, and exports the resulting table with
\code{sp.regtable}. \code{sp.datasets.card\_1995()} returns the public
\code{wooldridge::card} extract itself, so the session of
Listing~\ref{lst:motivating} runs on the original bytes.
Listing~\ref{lst:card-output} continues that session at a prompt and
prints what it produces.

The three linear-in-\code{educ} summaries land where the literature
puts them. \(\hat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}} = 0.07401\) compares with Card's
published 0.075, and
\(\hat\beta_{\mathrm{IV}}^{\mathrm{nearc4}} = 0.1323\) with his 0.132.
This reproduces the IV-above-OLS pattern that motivates the design, and
cross-fit DML agrees with OLS to the third decimal. \proglang{R}'s
\code{lm}+\pkg{sandwich} and \pkg{AER}\code{::ivreg} return the same
two coefficients on the same CSV bytes at relative errors
\(7.9\times 10^{-15}\) and \(1.6\times 10^{-11}\)
(Table~\ref{tab:headline-parity}).
The \code{sp.audit} block makes the audit-hook claim concrete. It
separates three states that a flat checklist would merge. The
first-stage \(F\) of 16.72 clears the \(F>10\) rule of thumb
\citep{staiger1997instrumental,stock2005testing}. Here ``passed'' means
only that this screen was met. It is neither a test of instrument
validity nor a guarantee against weak-instrument distortion, which is why
weak-instrument-robust Anderson--Rubin inference is still listed as
missing. The over-identification test is reported as not applicable, not
as missing. With one endogenous regressor and one excluded instrument the
model is just-identified, so the Sargan/Hansen statistic has zero degrees
of freedom and there is no restriction to test. The audit reads the
instrument and endogenous-regressor counts from the fitted object to
decide this. When an over-identified fit already carries a \(J\)
statistic, the audit reads and grades it. The text, JSON, and MCP
outputs carry the same \code{not\_applicable} status and reason.

The four estimators do not share one identification argument, and the
table should not be read as four estimates of one parameter. The 2SLS
coefficient uses \code{nearc4} as an excluded instrument and, with
heterogeneous returns, identifies a local average effect for
instrument-compliers under relevance, exclusion, independence, and
monotonicity. The DML and forest calls in Listing~\ref{lst:motivating}
use no instrument. They adjust for the listed covariates and are causal
only under conditional exogeneity of schooling, which is the assumption
the IV design exists to avoid. On these data they therefore estimate an
adjusted partial association, and the forest's heterogeneity is
heterogeneity in that association. The listing shows that the four fits
share one result contract. It does not identify heterogeneous returns to
schooling. That would require an IV-based heterogeneity estimator, with
its own validation. With that caveat, Listing~\ref{lst:card-cate}
summarises the fitted forest's conditional effects.

The Track~A fixture for this design is a calibrated replica, not the
extract above, which is why its Card rows
(Table~\ref{tab:track-a-cross-language-snapshot}) differ from the
original-data rows of Table~\ref{tab:headline-parity}. Because schooling
is continuous, the forest's scalar aggregate is the average partial

\code{cf.average\_treatment\_effect()} returns \(0.0787\) (SE

\subsection[Callaway-Sant'Anna staggered DiD: mpdta]{Callaway--Sant'Anna staggered DiD: \texttt{mpdta}}\label{sec:ex-csdid}

The \code{mpdta} example is the main panel-workflow demonstration.
Listing~\ref{lst:csdid} shows the full fit-to-sensitivity chain from one
fitted object.

% A float, so the listing and its caption always share a page (inline, a
% page break stranded the last argument line and the caption).
\begin{lstlisting}[float=tb,caption={Callaway--Sant'Anna fit, simple and
event-study aggregation, and Rambachan--Roth Honest-DiD bounds, all from
the same fitted object; the Goodman--Bacon decomposition, a diagnostic
of the two-way fixed-effects comparison weights, is computed from the
data.},label={lst:csdid},captionpos=b]
mpdta = sp.datasets.mpdta()
cs  = sp.callaway_santanna(mpdta, y="lemp", t="year", i="countyreal",
                           g="first_treat", control_group="nevertreated",
                           estimator="reg")
agg = sp.aggte(cs, type="simple", random_state=0)
es  = sp.aggte(cs, type="dynamic", random_state=0)
hd  = sp.honest_did(cs, e=0, method="smoothness")
bd  = sp.bacon_decomposition(mpdta, y="lemp", treat="treat",
                             time="year", id="countyreal")
\end{lstlisting}

\code{sp.callaway\_santanna} estimates group-time ATTs,
\code{sp.aggte} reports simple and dynamic aggregations,
\code{sp.honest\_did} applies the Rambachan--Roth sensitivity
post-estimator, and \code{sp.bacon\_decomposition} gives the TWFE
weight diagnostic \citep{goodmanbacon2021difference}.
Listing~\ref{lst:csdid-output} prints the two objects a reader of this
literature would want to see. One is the aggregated effect. The other is
how much parallel-trends violation the effect in the first treated
period survives. They are different estimands, and the transcript labels
them as such.

\begin{lstlisting}[float=tb,caption={Two estimands from the fit of
Listing~\ref{lst:csdid}: the simple aggregate over all post-treatment
periods, and the effect at event time \(e=0\) with its Rambachan--Roth
smoothness bounds. \code{M} bounds the change in slope of the
differential trend between consecutive periods (Equation~\ref{eq:delta-sd});
\code{rejects\_zero} is whether the robust confidence set still excludes
zero at that bound.},label={lst:csdid-output},captionpos=b]
>>> print(f"simple ATT, all post periods: {float(agg.estimate):.6f}"
...       f"  (SE {float(agg.se):.6f})")
simple ATT, all post periods: -0.032977  (SE 0.007961)
>>> print("ATT at e = 0, smoothness bounds:")

>>> print(hd.round(4).to_string(index=False))
     M  ci_lower  ci_upper  rejects_zero
0.0000   -0.0546   -0.0214          True
0.0037   -0.0566   -0.0129          True
0.0075   -0.0582   -0.0017          True
0.0112   -0.0601    0.0058         False
0.0149   -0.0632    0.0106         False
0.0224   -0.0706    0.0181         False
\end{lstlisting}

Writing \(\delta_t\) for the differential trend of treated and
comparison units in period \(t\) (in years), the smoothness restriction
of \citet{rambachan2023more} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta-sd}
  \Delta^{SD}(M) = \bigl\{\delta : \bigl|(\delta_{t+1}-\delta_t) -
  (\delta_t-\delta_{t-1})\bigr| \le M \ \text{for all } t\bigr\},
\end{equation}
so \(M\) bounds how much the slope of the differential trend may change
from one year to the next, in log points per year per year. \(M=0\)
allows only a linear continuation of the pre-treatment trend. On the
printed grid the first-period effect still excludes zero at
\(M = 0.0075\) but not at \(M = 0.0112\). A continuous search
(\code{sp.breakdown\_m}) puts the breakdown value at \(0.0080\). That
value is the quantity a referee should be given, more than the pre-trend
test's \(p\)-value, and it comes off the same fitted object as the point
estimate.

That last clause is the case for the shared contract, and it can be
measured. The Rambachan--Roth interval needs the \emph{joint} covariance
of the event-study coefficients, the relative times, and the position of
the reference period. Composed by hand, the workflow extracts these from
one package's aggregation object and re-enters them into another's
sensitivity function. A careful user of \pkg{did} and \pkg{HonestDiD}
passes the joint covariance, and nothing here suggests otherwise. The
problem is that the hand-off also offers a shortcut that runs without
complaint, which is to rebuild the covariance from the reported standard
errors. On \texttt{mpdta} the pre- and post-period coefficients are
correlated up to 0.61, so the shortcut matters. With
\(\operatorname{diag}(\mathrm{SE}^2)\) in place of the joint covariance,
the breakdown value rises from 0.0080 to 0.0101. The estimate would then
be reported as surviving trend deviations 26\% larger than the data
support, and the \(M=0\) interval would shift from
\([-0.0546,-0.0214]\) to \([-0.0477,-0.0156]\).

In \statspai{}, \code{sp.honest\_did} reads the joint covariance, the
relative times, and the aggregation weights from the fitted object
through one extractor, \code{sp.event\_study\_vcov}, which nine
event-study estimators share. When a fit cannot supply a joint
covariance, the function says so and falls back to a labelled
approximation. It does not fabricate one from standard errors. A single
entry point also means a correctness fix applies once. The 1.30.0 repair
of the cohort-share term in that covariance
(Table~\ref{tab:correctness-history}) reached every estimator's
sensitivity analysis at the same time. The saving is not in lines of
code. It is that the covariance, the reference period, and the estimand
travel with the estimate and are never re-typed. The
\code{random\_state=0} in Listing~\ref{lst:csdid} matters too.
\code{sp.aggte} follows \pkg{did} in defaulting to a 1{,}000-draw
multiplier bootstrap. The aggregated standard error is therefore a
random quantity, and a printed transcript of it is reproducible only
once the draw is pinned.

The parity evidence sits on two data sets, and the paper keeps them apart. On the
calibrated \texttt{mpdta} replica of Track~A module 04,
\statspai{}, \pkg{did}, and \code{csdid} all return the simple ATT
\(-0.0329765\) in the R-joined harness and agree within the registered
iterative tolerance on the analytic SE. On the \emph{original}
\code{did::mpdta} bytes, \statspai{} and \pkg{did} return the simple
ATT \(-0.03995\) at relative error \(2.3\times10^{-14}\) and the
vignette-printed overall dynamic ATT \(-0.0772\) at
\(1.6\times10^{-14}\) (Table~\ref{tab:headline-parity}). The
homogeneous calibrated
replica additionally checks that Callaway--Sant'Anna, Sun--Abraham
\citep{sun2021estimating}, BJS/imputation \citep{borusyak2024revisiting},
and DR-DiD \citep{santanna2020doubly} collapse to the same estimand when
their identification restrictions coincide.
Figure~\ref{fig:mpdta-es} is the printed event-study counterpart to
Listing~\ref{lst:csdid}.

\subsection[RD incumbency advantage (Lee-style sharp design)]{Regression discontinuity: the incumbency advantage (Lee-style sharp design)}\label{sec:ex-rd}

The RD example deliberately separates the local-polynomial estimator
from the bandwidth selector. It also keeps two data objects distinct, to
avoid over-claiming. The \emph{exact} cross-language parity anchor is
the public \code{rdrobust::RDsenate} extract. This is the U.S.\ Senate
margin-of-victory sample distributed with \pkg{rdrobust}. It is a
Lee-style sharp RD, but it is \emph{not} \citet{lee2008randomized}'s
original House data, which we do not redistribute. The calibrated
\code{lee\_2008\_senate()} replica is a redistribution-safe drift-guard
and not a parity target. On it \code{sp.rdrobust} returns a robust jump
of \(0.0763\), and the external test only asserts that the estimate
stays in the \([0.05,0.12]\) structural neighbourhood.
The native \code{sp.rdrobust} default implements the
\citet{calonico2014robust} CCT bandwidth cascade and robust
bias-corrected inference in \statspai{} itself
\citep[software article:][]{calonico2015rdrobust}. On the RDsenate
extract it returns the same MSE-optimal bandwidth (17.7544) and the same
conventional and robust estimates as \proglang{R}'s default
\pkg{rdrobust}, at relative error below \(3\times10^{-13}\)
(Section~\ref{sec:track-a}). Users who prefer the authors' own code can
call \code{sp.rdrobust(..., bwselect="cct")}, which delegates to the
official \pkg{rdrobust} \proglang{Python} port. The two agree to
\(2\times10^{-13}\). That agreement is recorded as a convergence check
and is not counted as parity. The previous
density-test selector gap is closed by the native \code{sp.rddensity}
port (Section~\ref{sec:track-a}).

\subsection{LaLonde (1986) / Dehejia--Wahba (1999): the NSW programme}\label{sec:ex-lalonde}

The LaLonde \citep{lalonde1986evaluating} / Dehejia--Wahba
\citep{dehejia1999causal} workflow shows the same mature result surface
under an experimental benchmark and an observational comparison. On
\code{causalsens::lalonde.psid} \citep{blackwell2014selection}, the naive OLS ATT is
\(-\$15{,}200\) in both \statspai{} and \proglang{R}, and the
covariate-adjusted OLS ATT is \(\$751.9\) on both sides. On
\code{MatchIt::lalonde}, 1:1 nearest-neighbour PSM agrees with
\pkg{MatchIt} up to the registered caliper/default convention. The
replication script also emits balance and overlap diagnostics. Matching
does not fix the benchmark here. What the example shows is that the
platform keeps the experimental anchor, the observational bias, and the
preprocessing diagnostics in one auditable pipeline.

\subsection{Causal time series: advertising lift}\label{sec:ex-causal-impact}

The causal-impact example uses a deterministic Brodersen-style
\citep{brodersen2015inferring} advertising-lift simulation rather than
proprietary marketing data.
The known post-intervention lift is \(4.2\) sales units per day;
\code{sp.causal\_impact} estimates \(4.306\) with SE \(0.140\), a 95\%
interval \([4.032,4.579]\), cumulative effect \(129.2\) against true
\(126.0\), and pre-period RMSE \(0.562\). These numbers are not
cross-language parity claims. They are known-truth guards, which check
that the time-series counterfactual machinery, the result object, and
the publication export path are wired correctly.

\subsection{Three ecosystems, one number: panel counterfactuals and interaction effects}\label{sec:ex-fect}

The last example shows the same estimator returning the same number in
all three languages. It uses two method families whose authors ship
both \proglang{R} and \proglang{Stata} implementations. The first is the
set of counterfactual estimators for time-series cross-sectional data of
\citet{liu2024practical}. In \pkg{fect} these are two-way fixed effects,
interactive fixed effects, and matrix completion, fitted on the
untreated cells. The second is the interaction-effect diagnostics of
\citet{hainmueller2019much}. In \pkg{interflex} these are linear,
binning, and kernel estimators of a conditional marginal effect.
\statspai{} ports both natively, down to \pkg{fect}'s
EM map and the linear-binning-plus-FFT density grid behind
\pkg{interflex}'s adaptive kernel bandwidth. Listing~\ref{lst:fect} runs
them on the panels of parity modules 86--87, after a \pkg{panelView}-style
look at the treatment pattern \citep{mou2023panel}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Panel counterfactuals and interaction
effects. \code{sp.panel\_view} reports adoption timing, reversals, and
missing cells before any model is fitted; \code{sp.fect} imputes the
untreated outcome of every treated cell under three outcome models;
\code{sp.interflex} estimates how the treatment effect varies with a
moderator and tests the linear-interaction restriction.},label={lst:fect},captionpos=b]
fig, ax, info = sp.panel_view(panel, unit="id", time="time",
                              treat="D")
info["staggered"], info["has_reversals"]       # (True, False)
fe  = sp.fect(panel, y="Y", treat="D", unit="id", time="time",
              covariates=["X1", "X2"], method="fe")
ife = sp.fect(panel, y="Y", treat="D", unit="id", time="time",
              covariates=["X1", "X2"], method="ife", r=2,
              vce="bootstrap", n_boot=200, seed=0)
mc  = sp.fect(panel, y="Y", treat="D", unit="id", time="time",
              covariates=["X1", "X2"], method="mc", lam=0.002)
ife.detail                              # ATT by relative period
bins = sp.interflex(sample, y="Y", d="D", x="X", z=["Z1"],
                    estimator="binning", cutoffs=[0.3, 1.7])
bins.model_info["tests"]["p_wald"]      # linear vs binning
kern = sp.interflex(sample, y="Y", d="D", x="X", z=["Z1"],
                    estimator="kernel", bw=1.0)

One scope boundary must be stated before the numbers. By default the
\proglang{R} \pkg{fect} and \pkg{interflex} packages select the factor
number \(r\), the matrix-completion penalty \(\lambda\), and the kernel
bandwidth by cross-validation, and cross-validation draws random folds.
The three-way parity claim below is therefore
established at \emph{user-specified} tuning values (the listing's
\code{r=2}, \code{lam=0.002}, and \code{bw=1.0} are the values fixed
by parity modules 86--87 so that all three implementations solve the
same problem). Since 1.29.0 the selectors themselves are
ported (\code{sp.fect(cv=True)}, \code{sp.interflex(cv=True)}). Their
folds are random, so they cannot be T2, and the evidence is a stochastic
screen rather than a T3 equivalence test. On identical folds the
per-fold scores equal \pkg{fect}'s to \(5.9\times10^{-8}\) and
\pkg{interflex}'s to \(2.8\times10^{-13}\). Across fold seeds (twenty in
\proglang{R}, five in \statspai{}) the \pkg{fect} selections coincide:
\(r=2\) and the same \(\lambda\) in every run on both sides. For the
\pkg{interflex} bandwidth, \proglang{R}'s own seeds do not agree. Its
twenty picks spread over six neighbouring grid points with no majority.
The check is therefore only that every \statspai{} pick lies on
\proglang{R}'s flat stretch of the loss curve, which it does. The mean CV
curves differ by less than four two-sample Monte Carlo standard errors
at every grid point
(\code{tests/reference\_parity/test\_fect\_interflex\_cv\_parity.py}).
The fit at a CV-selected value equals the fit at that value supplied
by hand, so the T2 rows above are unchanged by the port.

Table~\ref{tab:ex08-three-way} sets the \statspai{} numbers next to
the \proglang{R} and \proglang{Stata} goldens. On the same CSV bytes the
interactive-fixed-effects ATT agrees to \(4\times10^{-16}\) with
\proglang{R} and to \(3\times10^{-10}\) with \proglang{Stata}. The rows
that are deliberately not three-way are as informative as the rest. The
\proglang{Stata} \code{interflex} command uses a fixed Gaussian kernel.
It also tests the linear restriction without the covariate-by-bin terms
and against an \(F\) reference. \code{sp.interflex} exposes both
conventions (\code{adaptive=}, \code{wald\_full\_moderate=},
\code{wald\_test=}) and reproduces each reference to machine precision.
The complete row set (105 rows for \pkg{fect}, 25 for \pkg{interflex})
is in the parity ledger.
